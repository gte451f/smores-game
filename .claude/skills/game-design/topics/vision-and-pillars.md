# Vision and Pillars

## Vision

An open-world, squad-based survival and strategy game where the player starts with
nothing and earns relevance in a world that neither notices nor accommodates them.
Progress comes from accumulated knowledge of the world's systems — factions, economies,
dangers — and from the slow, fragile growth of a squad that improves only because it
survived long enough to learn.

Not a pure sandbox: the game offers a curated set of optional, advertised end-game
objectives for players who want a compass, while the world keeps existing and evolving
independent of any single victory. A player who ignores every objective and plays to
their own agenda is equally well supported.

## Player Fantasy

- **Starting with nothing and making something real** — desperate early survival growing
  into a self-sustaining operation with supply chains, trained personnel, and a footprint
  in the world.
- **Understanding a hostile world and turning it to your advantage** — faction tensions,
  trade shortages, and political shifts are readable patterns a knowledgeable player can
  exploit.
- **Watching individuals become specialists** — a recruit who nearly died on their first
  patrol becomes a best field commander because they kept getting back up, and their wages
  reflect it. Keeping a capable squad fielded is a continuous economic commitment, not a
  one-time cost.
- **Building something that persists** — a base is infrastructure, defense, and
  production; a statement that the player intends to stay.
- **Operating in the shadows** — stealth, pickpocketing, theft, fencing, kidnapping, and
  the slave trade are fully supported playstyles. A squad can survive and profit entirely
  through illicit means if the player is skilled and careful.

## The Seven Design Pillars

1. **Earned Progress** — nothing is given. Competence (including illicit skills) reflects
   real accumulated risk, not a level-up screen. A veteran squad is also an expensive one.
2. **Consequence** — actions leave lasting marks: injuries, faction standing, the fate of
   faction leaders. Illicit acts follow the same rule — getting caught costs standing and
   can trigger pursuit; running a criminal operation is about managing exposure, not just
   skill.
3. **Unscripted Narrative** — there is no authored story. The player's narrative emerges
   from systems colliding: a shortage becomes an opportunity, a betrayal becomes an enemy.
   No two campaigns should feel the same.
4. **Living World** — the simulation runs whether or not the player is watching. Factions
   expand, collapse, and negotiate; trade routes open and close; other squads compete for
   the same resources and territory. The player is one actor among many.
5. **Squad Identity** — characters are individuals with unique skill histories, injuries,
   prosthetics, and specializations. Losing a veteran is a real setback, not a respawn.
6. **Systems Mastery as Power** — the path from weak to strong runs through
   understanding, not grinding. Reading the economy, exploiting faction rivalries, timing
   expansion to regional stability, and equipping for a specific threat beats simply
   playing more hours.
7. **Optional End-Game Objectives** — a defined, advertised set of win conditions the
   sandbox does not require. Each should be ambitious enough that completing it represents
   mastery of multiple interlocking systems, and each should have at least one "hard mode"
   variant that must be met under active pressure. Categories (specifics deferred until
   setting/systems mature): economic dominance, military/political ascendancy,
   self-sufficiency, territorial presence, legacy. Community-contributed objectives should
   be supportable through modding.

## What This Game Deliberately Is Not

- **Not a city builder** — the player is always in the field; the base serves the squad.
  The per-character job queues in `orders-and-jobs.md` are the closest any system comes to
  this line; that topic states where the boundary sits and why it holds.
- **Not a traditional RPG** — no single hero; character death is recoverable at the squad
  level but costly.
- **Not an RTS or turn-based tactics game** — combat is real-time and automated;
  characters fight on their own. The player directs movement, target priority, and
  engage/retreat decisions, not individual attacks.
- **Not a 4X or faction management game** — the player cannot own towns, set taxes,
  control trade routes, or declare war as a political entity. Influence comes through
  reputation, squad capability, and economic activity, not territorial sovereignty (see
  `factions-and-world-state.md`).
- **Not a power fantasy** — the game should feel dangerous at every stage; late-game
  strength is relative to late-game threats.

## Tone and Difficulty Philosophy

Punishing by design. Early failure is expected and instructive; systems are learnable but
not hand-held. No difficulty sliders in the core experience — the world behaves
consistently and the player adapts to it.

- **Failure should be informative.** A wiped patrol should be explainable in hindsight.
  Failure from invisible systems or poor feedback is a design bug; failure from
  underestimating a known danger is correct behavior.
- **Recovery should always be possible.** Even a catastrophic loss — base destroyed, squad
  gutted — should leave some path forward, however steep. Soft-locking the player through
  opaque systems is a failure mode to avoid.

## Campaign Time Scale

A campaign spans **1–5 in-game years**, with one in-game day ≈ 40 real-time minutes
(current baseline, may shrink further). Time is deliberately compressed — crop cycles,
research, and skill growth all move faster than realistic — so that systems feel
meaningful within the campaign window rather than a human historical timescale. No
generational mechanics; characters don't age out of a campaign.

## Differentiation at a Glance

A few of the choices that most define the game's identity relative to its closest
comparison point, Kenshi:

- Squad members are hired for a fee **and** an ongoing, skill-scaled wage — a deliberate
  currency sink, not a one-time cost.
- The map is **static and handcrafted**, same geography every playthrough, dense with
  authored points of interest — not procedurally generated.
- The player can build **one active outpost** plus purchasable town buildings, never
  multiple freeform bases.
- **No player faction status, ever** — no town ownership, taxation, or trade route
  control, regardless of squad size or power.
- Illicit play is a fully supported playstyle but is **constrained** — detection, security
  escalation, stolen-goods recognition, fencing requirements, consistent outcomes on
  reload — rather than an exploitable gear-skip.
- The game ships with **optional, advertised end-game objectives**; pure sandbox play
  remains equally valid.
